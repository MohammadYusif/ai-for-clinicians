# Day One — talking points

Instructor script for Day One, Foundations: 120 minutes including the 10-minute break, in the order of `course/timing.json`. Clock cues run from the start of each topic; the class clock is in each status line. Slide `Notes:` are plain text under 900 characters, ready to paste into the deck's speaker notes. Every case is a synthetic [case card](../reference/case-cards.qmd). No real patient appears in class or in this file, and example outputs shown in class are written for the course, never captured from a tool.

## Topic 2 — The rule for the weekend (5 min)

[Participant page](../day1/m1-rule-and-mechanics.qmd#rule) · Class clock 0:05–0:10 · Deck: kept, `rule`, no changes.

**Goal.** The room can say the rule from memory and give one example of what "you decide" covers that a tool cannot.

**Slides**

1. `rule` — kept. Headline: "AI drafts. You decide." On-slide: "The Rule for the Weekend"; "AI drafts. You decide."; "Every tool. Every task. All three days." Visual: full-bleed indigo `#4F46E5`, 132 px white headline on two lines, centered, small-caps label above, subline in `#E0E7FF` below.
   Notes: This is the sentence for the whole weekend. AI drafts: it can draft, structure, summarize and rephrase, quickly. You decide: you choose what is true, you verify it against the chart or a source, and you sign. Accountability stays with the physician. That is a statement about professional responsibility, not legal advice; for a legal question, ask your institution's data protection officer or legal team. Three applications: a note, a referral, a handout. One violation: pasting the output without reading it. Every tool. Every task. All three days.

**Say**

- 0:00–0:45. Put the rule up, read it aloud, and stop talking for three seconds. This is the one sentence to keep if you keep nothing else.
- 0:45–1:45. Two columns (Do step 2). The tool: draft, structure, summarize, rephrase. You: decide, verify, sign. Nothing in the second column can be handed over.
- 1:45–2:45. Accountability stays with the physician: whatever carries your name, a chart entry, a letter, a handout, is yours, and the tool's part in the first version does not move that. Say once that this is professional responsibility, not legal advice.
- 2:45–3:45. Three jobs, one violation, from the page's table: check the numbers, medicines and plan of a note; the question, urgency and allergy of a referral; the red flags of a handout, which come from you. The violation is not using the tool. It is pasting without reading.
- 3:45–4:45. The page's Try it (Do step 3).
- 4:45–5:00. Bridge.

**Do**

1. Before class: `rule` ready; a whiteboard or flip chart with two columns headed "AI drafts" and "You decide".
2. Ask: "What does the tool do well?" then "What can it never do for you?" File 60 seconds of answers under the two columns; diagnose, verify, decide and sign go under "You decide". Leave the columns up for the Lab One debrief.
3. Try it, one minute: "Think of the last thing you asked an AI tool to write. Which half did you do: the draft or the decision? Tell your neighbor in one sentence." Take one or two answers if the room is willing; do not push.

**If asked**

- **If it errs and I miss it, am I liable?** I cannot give legal advice. The course teaches the professional stance: you are responsible for what carries your name. For the legal position, ask your institution's data protection officer or legal team.
- **Isn't this what I already do with a resident's draft?** Yes, the stance is the same. What differs is that the tool's draft sounds equally sure whether it is right or wrong. That is the next topic.
- **What about tools that write the note for me?** Same rule, the note is still read line by line. Day Two covers ambient scribes.

**Watch for**

- "You decide" heard as "do it all yourself". The rule is not against the tool; the drafting is where the time comes back.
- "Decide" heard as "click accept". Deciding includes verifying.
- A request for a legal conclusion. Decline once, kindly, and point to the data protection officer or legal team.
- Scolding. The violation example is pasting without reading, not using AI.

**Bridge.** "To decide well you need to know what you are deciding about: what these tools actually do when they answer."

## Topic 3 — How these tools actually work (10 min)

[Participant page](../day1/m1-rule-and-mechanics.qmd#how-it-works) · Class clock 0:10–0:20 · Deck: new, four slides (`next-word`, `sure-not-right`, `what-it-knows`, `six-habits`). No case card: the exercise is a plain sentence completion.

**Goal.** The room can explain, without jargon, why a fluent answer can be wrong, and name what the tool does and does not know.

**Slides**

1. `next-word` — new. Headline: "It Predicts the Next Word". On-slide: three fill-in-the-blank lines, revealed one at a time: "Twinkle, twinkle, little ___"; "Take one tablet twice a ___"; "The guideline that covers this was published in ___". Visual: three large lines in 44 px DM Sans stacked on the light ground, each blank an indigo underscore. The lines are the ones in the participant page's Try it, so the room can follow either.
   Notes: Read the first line and let the room finish it together. Star, at once. Second line: take one tablet twice a. Day, almost everyone says, yet nobody said which drug, and the room just completed a dosing instruction. Third line: the guideline that covers this was published in. You will hear different years, all plausible, none checked. That is the mechanism: the tool continues text with what is likely, given everything before it. Very fast autocomplete. Most of the time likely and true line up. When they do not, the tool has no step that notices.

2. `sure-not-right` — new. Headline: "Sounding Sure Is Not Being Right". On-slide: "Fluent is what it was built to produce."; "No built-in 'I'm unsure' signal."; "Tone is not a signal." Visual: two identical confident speech-bubble cards side by side, indigo border, teal caption "Same tone either way."
   Notes: Fluent, confident prose is what these tools were built to produce. Right or wrong, the sentence reads the same. There is no built-in unsure signal, no warning light, no change in tone when the tool is guessing. You will hear the word hallucination for this; I will say made-up or fabricated, because that is what it is: text that reads as plausible and is false. The lesson is not to distrust everything. It is that tone tells you nothing, so the checking has to come from you. That is what the three questions are for, and we build them in a few minutes.

3. `what-it-knows` — new. Headline: "What It Knows". On-slide: "The conversation: what you typed and pasted."; "Its training: general text, up to a cutoff."; "Not: your patient, your protocol, or news after the cutoff, unless it searches." Visual: three cards in a row, indigo, teal, and a dashed grey card for the "Not" item.
   Notes: Two sources only. The conversation: whatever you typed or pasted in this chat. And its training: a very large amount of general text, up to a cutoff date. It has no memory of your other chats unless a memory feature is switched on. It does not know your patient, your hospital's protocol, or anything after its cutoff. It has no internet unless the tool searches. Whatever you leave out, it fills with plausible, generic content, which is where most made-up detail comes from. Tools that search and cite change part of this picture; we look at them in Module 3, later today.

4. `six-habits` — new. Headline: "Six Habits That Follow". On-slide, three cards with two habits each: "Give it the facts. Ask neutrally."; "Expect different answers. New task, new chat."; "Check every number. Every output is a draft." Visual: three numbered cards, indigo numerals 01 to 03, 30 px text.
   Notes: Six habits, from what you just heard. Give it the facts, because it knows nothing about your patient. Ask neutrally: a leading question gets an agreeable answer. Expect different answers to the same question; that is normal, not a fault. Start a fresh chat for a new task, so old context does not carry in. Check every number: doses, values and dates are where errors cost most. Treat every output as a draft, which is the rule again. Two analogies that hold: very fast autocomplete, and a well-read colleague who has never examined your patient. One that does not: a database of facts, or a search engine.

**Say**

- 0:00–0:30. Frame: five minutes of plain language, no jargon. Everything later this weekend leans on it.
- 0:30–3:00. `next-word`. Run the exercise (Do step 2). Then: these tools are very fast autocomplete. Plausible is not the same as correct.
- 3:00–4:30. `sure-not-right`. Fluent is what it was built to produce, and there is no built-in "I'm unsure" signal. Say "hallucination" once, as the word they will hear elsewhere. Here we say made-up or fabricated.
- 4:30–6:30. `what-it-knows`. The conversation plus its training. No memory of other chats unless a feature is on; no knowledge of your patient or protocol; a training cutoff; no internet unless the tool searches. What you leave out, it fills with something plausible. Preview: tools that search and cite come in Module 3.
- 6:30–9:00. `six-habits`. Three pairs, each with a clinical consequence: a leading question invites a yes; a new task in an old chat carries old context along.
- 9:00–9:30. Two analogies that hold: very fast autocomplete; a well-read colleague who has never examined your patient. One that does not: a database of facts, or a search engine.
- 9:30–10:00. Bridge.

**Do**

1. Nothing live and no tool open: the point is intuition, and the live demo comes in Topic 11.
2. Sentence completion, two minutes; nobody is tested. Read each line and stop; the room says the next word aloud. (a) "Twinkle, twinkle, little ..." comes out the same for everyone, at once. (b) "Take one tablet twice a ..." comes just as easily, yet nobody said which drug, and the room just completed a dosing instruction. (c) "The guideline that covers this was published in ..." gets different years, all plausible, none checked. Close: plausible is not the same as correct.
3. Fallback if the room is quiet: answer (a) yourself, then invite the room on (b) and (c).

**If asked**

- **Does it learn from what I type?** It depends on the tool and its settings, and I will not state a product's policy from memory. Treat anything typed into a consumer tool as leaving your control. That is the privacy line; Day Three explains it.
- **Why a different answer the second time?** Answers are generated fresh each time, so differences are normal. It is also why one run is never a measurement. The live demo returns to this.
- **Does it actually understand medicine?** It produces text that reads as though it does. The practical point is narrower: nothing in it checks the facts against your patient, so you do.

**Watch for**

- "So it's just autocomplete, so it's useless." The frame explains the failure mode; it is not a dismissal. Drafting is where the tool earns its place.
- The opposite: "It knows more medicine than I do." It has read about medicine. It has not examined your patient. Question three is about exactly this.
- Technical rabbit holes (tokens, parameters, training). Park them until after class.

**Bridge.** "A draft that sounds sure and can be wrong needs a check that does not depend on tone. You get three questions."

## Topic 4 — Your three-question check (5 min)

[Participant page](../day1/m1-rule-and-mechanics.qmd#three-questions) · Reference card: [three-question-check](../reference/three-question-check.qmd) · Class clock 0:20–0:25 · Deck: kept, `gutcheck`, plus one new slide, `altered-dose`. Case A supplies the source line.

**Goal.** The room can recite the three questions in order, say what each one really asks, and use them on a draft with one changed dose.

**Slides**

1. `gutcheck` — kept. Headline: "Your Three-Question Check". On-slide: (1) three cards: "Could I defend this to a colleague?", "Does it point to something I can verify?", "Would I catch it if it were wrong?" (2) "If the answer to any of these is no, that's the signal to slow down, not the finding to accept." Visual: white cards with Chat (indigo), Search (teal), Warning (indigo) icons, 30 px semibold text; the no rule in 28 px slate below.
   Notes: Three questions, in this order. Could I defend this to a colleague? That asks whether I could explain the reasoning to a peer, not whether it sounds right. Does it point to something I can verify? A source I can actually open, or a value in the chart. Would I catch it if it were wrong? That asks whether I have the expertise to notice the error; if not, this task is not for this tool. If the answer to any of these is no, that's the signal to slow down, not the finding to accept. The check scales with the stakes: a brainstorm needs a glance, a dose in a letter needs the chart open.

2. `altered-dose` — new. Headline: "Run the Check". On-slide: (1) source, from the Case A card: "add losartan 50 mg once daily, keep amlodipine 5 mg, repeat creatinine and potassium in 2 weeks" (2) illustrative draft: "P: Start losartan 50 mg twice daily. Continue amlodipine 5 mg once daily. Repeat creatinine and potassium in 2 weeks." (3) "Ten seconds: what changed?" Visual: before/after, two cards side by side, source in grey, draft with an indigo border and a small tag "Illustrative, written for this course, not from a real tool". On the click, the words "twice daily" turn teal. The draft line is the one on the participant page.
   Notes: Two lines. On the left, the plan from the Case A card. On the right, an illustrative draft written for this course, not captured from a tool. Give the room ten seconds to find the difference. Losartan 50 mg once daily became twice daily. Question one: could I defend it? Not this line; twice daily is not what I decided. Question two: does it point to something I can verify? Yes, my own plan, so the check is a comparison, not a search. Question three: would I catch it? Only if I compare line by line; read for sense and it is perfectly plausible. This is altered. The other two ways are invented and omitted.

**Say**

- 0:00–0:30. You now know the tool sounds sure whether it is right or wrong. Here is a check that does not depend on tone.
- 0:30–2:00. `gutcheck`. Read each question in the exact wording, then what it really asks: defend, could you explain your reasoning to a peer; verify, a source you can open or a value in the chart; catch, do you have the expertise to notice, and if not, the task is not for this tool. Then the no rule, word for word.
- 2:00–3:30. `altered-dose`. Run Do steps 1 to 3.
- 3:30–4:20. Name the failure: this one is "altered"; the other two are "invented" and "omitted". Stakes: a brainstormed title gets a glance; a slide or an email to a colleague is read in full; anything in a chart, any dose, date or result is checked line by line against the source. The check returns in Lab One part 2, Lab Three and the Day Three back-to-the-rule session.
- 4:20–5:00. Bridge.

**Do**

1. Before class: `altered-dose` staged with the source line only; the Case A card open in a tab.
2. Reveal the draft line. "Ten seconds, silently: what is different?" Take one answer.
3. Walk the three questions on it. Note that the change was found by comparing with the source: question two doing its job.
4. Point them to the one-page card and suggest they keep it open in the labs.

**If asked**

- **Do I run this on everything?** Scale it to the stakes. A brainstorm gets a glance; a dose, a diagnosis or a citation in a letter gets the source open.
- **What if there is no source to check?** Then question two is a no, which is the signal to slow down: find the source, or do not use the claim.
- **Can I ask the tool "are you sure?"** That is not a check. The check is yours, against something outside the tool.

**Watch for**

- Question three heard as self-doubt. It is a scope test: a task where you would not recognize the error is not one to hand over.
- The reveal landing as a gotcha. Keep it curious: the difference is easy to miss because the sentence reads well.
- People skipping the exact wording. They will need it in the labs; say it the same way every time this weekend.

**Bridge.** "Now the other half of the problem: why do vague prompts give you a wall of extra words to delete?"

## Topic 5 — Where the extra words come from (5 min)

[Participant page](../day1/m2-prompting.qmd#extra-words) · Class clock 0:25–0:30 · Deck: kept, `extra-words`, plus one new slide, `two-runs`. No live tool.

**Goal.** The room can explain why a vague prompt returns hedged, padded, differently shaped text, and that the fix is in the prompt.

**Slides**

1. `extra-words` — kept. Headline: "Where the Extra Words Come From". On-slide: (1) "A vague prompt gets a vague answer: hedged, padded, and inconsistent in structure." (2) "The fix isn't a better model. It's a better prompt." Visual: light ground, two 52 px statements stacked, the second in indigo.
   Notes: A vague prompt gets a vague answer: hedged, padded, inconsistent in structure. Here is why. Every choice you leave open, the audience, the length, the structure, the tone, the tool fills with the most typical pattern. The most typical pattern for a clinical answer includes hedges, padding and a generic layout, so that is what comes back. Not because the tool is broken. Because the decisions were left to it. The fix is not a better model. It is a better prompt, and the next few minutes are about what goes in one.

2. `two-runs` — new. Headline: "Same Vague Prompt, Two Runs". On-slide: (1) Run 1, prose: "It is important to note that ... may indicate ... Please consult the treating physician." (2) Run 2, headings: "Patient Summary. Key Findings. Considerations. Suggested Next Steps. Disclaimer." (3) "Left open: audience, length, structure, tone." Visual: two white cards side by side with the hedge phrases in bold indigo, a teal strip beneath for the "left open" line, and a small tag "Illustrative, written for this course. Not from a real tool." Wording follows the participant page's two runs.
   Notes: Same vague prompt, the Case A facts pasted underneath, two runs. These are illustrative, written for this course, not captured from any tool and not a measurement. Run one is prose full of stock hedges, and it ends by telling the treating physician to consult the treating physician: the tool guessed the audience and guessed wrong. Run two is headings ending in a disclaimer, and it turns the real plan on the card into medication adjustment. Different shapes, because nothing said which. The words look careful and say nothing. What was left open: audience, length, structure, tone.

**Say**

- 0:00–0:30. Ask: "When a wall of text comes back, what do you delete first?" Take two answers; expect disclaimers and openers.
- 0:30–1:45. `extra-words`. Every choice you leave open, the tool fills with the most typical pattern: typical hedges, typical padding, a typical layout.
- 1:45–3:15. `two-runs`. Say twice that these are illustrative, written for the course. Run 1 tells the treating physician to consult the treating physician: wrong audience. Run 2 turns the real plan on the card into "medication adjustment". The words look careful and say nothing. A hedge is not a safety check; the real risk is the long, padded draft you skim, because skimming is how a changed dose gets through.
- 3:15–4:15. The page's Try it (Do step 3).
- 4:15–5:00. The fix is not a better model; it is a better prompt. Do not teach the framework yet. Bridge.

**Do**

1. Before class: the Case A card open in a tab, so you can say "this is what someone would paste under a vague prompt".
2. Show `two-runs`: "Which line would you delete first? Who chose the length? The structure?" Nobody did.
3. Try it, one minute: "Think of the last thing you typed into an assistant. Which of the four did you leave open: audience, length, structure or tone? Tell your neighbor."

**If asked**

- **Won't a better model fix this?** I cannot speak for every model. What I can say is that whatever you leave open, the tool fills, on any model.
- **Why does it hedge so much?** The most typical wording for a clinical answer includes hedges and disclaimers. Unless you say otherwise, that is what returns.
- **Can't I just say "be brief"?** That is the start: length is one choice. Structure, audience and what to avoid matter too, which is the next topic.
- **Should I regenerate until it is right?** Regenerating gives a different draft, not a better-specified one. Fix the prompt.

**Watch for**

- The snippets heard as real output of a named product. Keep the label, and never say "ChatGPT does this".
- "The tool is bad." The lesson is the choices left open.
- Running long. This is problem framing; the fix is the next topic.

**Bridge.** "So what should you specify? Four things."

## Topic 6 — Anatomy of a good prompt (10 min)

[Participant page](../day1/m2-prompting.qmd#anatomy) · Class clock 0:30–0:40 · Deck: new, three slides (`four-parts`, `skeleton`, `spot-the-gap`). Prompt library slug: `four-part-skeleton`.

**Goal.** The room can name the four parts, say what each is for, and find the missing part in a weak prompt.

**Slides**

1. `four-parts` — new. Headline: "The Four Parts". On-slide: one row of four labels with captions: Role, who it is; Context, what it knows; Format, what shape to return; Constraints, what to avoid. Visual: four connected cards in a row, indigo and teal alternating; DM Sans 30 px labels, 24 px captions.
   Notes: Four parts. Role: who the AI is writing as, and for whom. It sets register and audience; it does not add knowledge, so a world-class cardiologist does not make the facts better. Context: the facts it may use, and what the output is for. Only the facts you give it. Format: the exact shape, template, headings, length. Constraints: what it must not do: no invented facts, no hedging, the units, the reading level. Memory line: who it is, what it knows, what shape to return, what to avoid.   
   ```text
   ROLE: You are [role], writing for [audience].
   CONTEXT: [What this is for.] Use ONLY the facts below. If something needed is missing, write [MISSING: what] instead of guessing.
   FACTS:
   [paste the facts]
   FORMAT: [Exact template or headings.] Maximum [N] words.
   CONSTRAINTS: Do not add findings, doses, or diagnoses that are not in the facts. No hedging phrases. [Units, reading level, abbreviations.]
   ```

   Notes: This is the skeleton. It is on the site and in the prompt library. FACTS is not a fifth part; it is the slot under Context where your facts go. The line to remember is in Context: use only the facts below; if something needed is missing, write MISSING and say what, instead of guessing. On-slide: prompts 1 to 3 from the page's Try it, verbatim, one per card: (1) the family physician writing for a cardiologist; (2) the pediatrician writing for a baby's parents; (3) the hospital physician writing for the family doctor. On-slide: (1) "You can conclude: what each tool showed you (a source, a limit, or nothing); how much checking each answer needs; which tool you would open first for this kind of question." (2) "You cannot conclude: that one tool is better; that a citation means the answer is right; that it holds in your specialty." (3) "A citation you have not opened is a claim you have not checked." Visual: two columns, the "can" column with a teal top border and the "cannot" column with an indigo one; the third line beneath.
   Notes: What you can conclude from one run: what each tool showed you, a source, a limit, or nothing; how much checking each answer needs before you would use it; and which tool you would reach for first for this kind of question. What you cannot conclude: that one tool is better or more accurate, because one question, one run, one day is not a benchmark, and the same tool can answer the same question differently next time; that a citation means the answer is right, because a citation you have not opened is a claim you have not checked; or that the result holds in your own specialty. In the lab you run your own comparison.

**Say**

- 0:00–0:30. Show the question on `demo-grid`. General, no patient information, the same words to both tools.
- 0:30–1:00. Paste into both, press Enter, start the timer.
- 1:00–2:30. Narrate while they generate. Read the five grid rows aloud and say what you are watching for. Do not read either answer yet.
- 2:30–4:15. Compare, one tool at a time, and fill the grid by show of hands.
- 4:15–5:00. `one-run`. What you can and cannot conclude. In Lab One part 3 they do this on their own screen.

**Do**

Before class, not counted in the five minutes:

1. A tool like Vera Health is for healthcare professionals, so if you are not one, use the other two, or ask a clinician co-presenter or a participant to run that side. Test both today, from the venue network.
2. The question in a note, ready to paste, in the wording of the `evidence-question` prompt from Lab One part 3: the question, then "Summarize the main options and how strong the evidence is for each. Cite sources I can open. If you cannot find a source, say so. This is a general question: no patient details." Use a question from your own field that you can judge, and not the Lab One default (the atrial fibrillation question), so the room's own run stays fresh. [TRAINER TO CONFIRM: the demo question.]
3. Rehearse once. Screenshot both full answers, with no account name or email showing, and note the date. Keep them on your laptop, not in the repository.

The five minutes:

1. 0:00. Show the question. Say it once: no patient information.
2. 0:30. Paste into the general assistant, then into the second option (for a literature search, type the question's key terms). Enter in both. Start the timer.
3. 1:00. While they generate, read the grid rows aloud. Say what you are watching for. Say nothing about either tool yet.
4. 2:30. Compare. General assistant first: sources you can open? limits stated? your question or a nearby one? Then the second option. Fill the grid by show of hands. Open one citation live, for 60 seconds at most, and read whether it says what the sentence says.
5. 4:15. Switch to `one-run` and close. Do not name a winner.

Fallback:

- If a tool has not returned by 2:00, or is down, switch to your rehearsal screenshots. Say so: "This is a rehearsal run from [date], same question, same prompt." The grid teaches the comparison, not the result.
- If only one tool fails, use its screenshots for that column and live output for the other. If the general assistant is down, use another general assistant you have signed in.
- If both fail, run the grid on the screenshots. Do not improvise a claim about either tool.

**If asked**

- **Which one won?** It is not a benchmark: one question, one run, one day. What matters is what each let you check.
- **The general assistant gave a citation. Is it real?** Open it. A citation you have not opened is a claim you have not checked. A made-up citation looks exactly like a real one.
- **The second option cited papers. So is it correct?** A citation is something to open, not proof. A tool can misread a source.

**Watch for**

- A horse race. The room wants a winner; refuse it kindly.
- The nicer formatting winning applause. Judge by what you can open.
- A citation that will not open. That is data, not an embarrassment; say so.
- Adding patient detail "to make it realistic". Do not.

## Lab 1 — facilitation

**Slides**

   Notes: Lab One, thirty minutes, four parts. Part one, twelve minutes: draft the same Case A note twice, once with a one-line prompt and once with the four-part framework, and time both. Part two, eight minutes: ask a tool for something it cannot reliably know, watch it answer confidently, run the three questions, and save the answer with the practice-output line, because Lab Three reuses it. Part three, five minutes: the same general question in a general assistant and in a second option that shows sources. Then a five-minute debrief. Case A is the only patient in this lab. **Before class**

- Deck: `lab1` staged after the break. A visible countdown timer set for the four boxes: 12, 8, 5, 5.

**Materials**

The lab page and the Case A card on the projector; the page's Worksheet, to print or copy; a countdown timer; a stopwatch and a notes app for every participant; your Topic 11 rehearsal screenshots as a fallback; the "AI drafts / You decide" columns from Topic 2 for the debrief.

**Minute by minute**

- **Part 1, Template test (1:30–1:42).** 0:00–1:30 set-up: Case A only; two fresh chats, same tool; one prompt, no re-prompting; record minutes, words, edits and errors (invented, omitted, altered). 1:30–6:00 Round A, one line plus the facts. 6:00–10:30 Round B, the four-part prompt in a new chat. 10:30–12:00 fill the table and write one sentence on what the template changed. Say at 6:00: "New chat."
- **Part 2, Break it on purpose (1:42–1:50).** 0:00–1:00 the aim is to see how confident a wrong answer sounds, not to catch the tool out. Pick one probe of five; fill the brackets from your own field so you can judge; probe 5, the drug interaction, is the highest-risk, so choose a less common pair you know well and never act on the output. 1:00–3:00 run it; if the tool declines, note it, then push once. 3:00–6:30 the three questions; for question 2, two minutes to find one item independently. 6:30–8:00 save as `lab1-practice-output` with the exact header line `PRACTICE OUTPUT: CONTAINS DELIBERATELY UNRELIABLE CONTENT. NOT FOR CLINICAL USE.`, one line on the probe and what they found, and a verdict: trust, verify first, or discard.
- **Part 3, Tool face-off (1:50–1:55).** 0:00–1:00 ask both the same question (the `evidence-question` prompt; the Case C question if they have none; for a plain literature search, the question's key terms). 1:00–4:00 fill the grid and time each tool. 4:00–5:00 one line: which would you trust, and for what. Not a benchmark.
- **Debrief (1:55–2:00).** 0:00–1:30 neighbors share the one prompt or change that worked best. 1:30–3:30 two or three share with the room. 3:30–4:30 add it to the personal library: title, prompt, check. 4:30–5:00 back to the "AI drafts / You decide" columns from Topic 2, then: bring your favorite prompt to Day Two, and keep `lab1-practice-output` for Lab Three.

**What good looks like**

- Part 1: both columns filled; at least one content fix, not just formatting, in each round; one sentence on what the template changed. Round B capped the words, so edits and errors say more than word count.
- Part 2: one saved answer carrying the header line, one item they tried to verify, and a verdict.
- Part 3: five rows scored, both times recorded, one trust line, no winner declared.
- Debrief: every participant can name one prompt they are keeping and the line they check in its output.

**Common problems and fixes**

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| No literature-grounded tool, or Vera Health will not open | Nothing depends on it: use the assistant's search or citation mode, or a literature search such as PubMed. |
| No general assistant account | Pair with a neighbor who has one. They still fill the grid from the shared screen. |
| "I found nothing to fix" | Read the plan lines against the card once more: drug names and doses, the two-week lab recheck, the four-week review, and whether the ankle swelling is shown as new since amlodipine. |
| The tool declines the probe | That is a good answer; note it. Push once with the line on the page, then try another probe. |
| They re-prompt in Round B | Record the first draft first. Re-prompting can wait until after the lab. |
| A real patient detail appears | Stop, remove it, go back to Case A. No shaming; say the privacy line once. |
| Part 1 overruns by two minutes | Trim question 2's independent check from two minutes to one. Never cut the save step, which Lab Three needs, or the debrief. |
| Phone only | The page works on a phone: use the phone's stopwatch and notes app. |

**Debrief prompts**

- Which of the four parts made the most difference for you, and how do your numbers show it?
- What was the most confident thing you could not check, or found wrong?
- Which of the three questions was hardest to answer, and why?
- In the face-off, which tool did you open first, and what would you still check?
- What is the one line you check in the output of the prompt you are keeping?

**Watch for**

- Anyone reading a result as "the tool is X% better". There is no such number; the room's numbers are theirs.
- The saved wrong answer losing its header line. It must never pass for a right one.
- Probe 5 answered fluently and trusted. The lesson is that fluency told them nothing; only the check did.
